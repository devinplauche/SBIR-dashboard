The short version

Two openings are live, and one of them is a trap

The 56 DoD topics closing 23 September look like the obvious target. They are not reachable — a new entity cannot complete SAM.gov registration in 19 days. Treat them as reference material only.

The real openings are the 48 DoD topics in pre-release (proposals accepted 23 Sep – 21 Oct) and NSF's rolling Project Pitch. Both are reachable if registration starts this week. Inside the DoD batch there are two topics that read almost like they were scoped for a small team doing LLM evaluation work.

Do this before 22 September

Pre-release is the only period when you may email a DoD topic author directly. Once proposals open on 23 Sep, contact is cut off entirely. A ten-minute call now is worth more than a week of proposal polish later — and almost nobody outside the incumbent base makes it.

Feasibility

Registration is the gate, not the writing

Every deadline below has to be read against one fixed cost: a new company needs roughly three to four weeks to clear SAM.gov, because DLA entity validation sits in the middle of it and cannot be accelerated. The UEI arrives in a day or two; the CAGE code is what you wait on.

The 23 September batch falls inside the registration window, which is what puts it out of reach. Everything from 1 October onward is live territory.

One knock-on effect worth planning around: NSF requires an invited Project Pitch before you may submit a full proposal, and pitch review takes one to two months. A pitch sent today most likely will not clear in time for 4 November. The honest NSF target is the 4 March 2027 deadline, with the pitch going in now.

Why it fits

The topic explicitly requires the testbed to work from queries and outputs alone, with no access to model internals. That is precisely the constraint you already operate under with a commercial model, and it removes the usual barrier of needing privileged infrastructure. Milestones are staged at months 2, 6, and 9 — market mechanics, then agent decision space, then demonstration. This is a software project end to end.

What makes it hard

DARPA is the most competitive component here, and the topic cites current literature by name — Magentic Marketplace among others. Reviewers will expect you to know that work cold. No listed topic author, so you cannot pre-brief this one.

Why it fits

The topic states outright that it wants proof by demonstration, not a full product build — an unusually honest scoping that plays to a small team's strengths. The core asks are a decision schema treating assumptions, risks, and bias checks as first-class objects, plus an agentic layer producing reproducible runs with explicit “what flips the decision” logic. And it has a named, reachable topic author right now.

What makes it hard

It assumes fluency in digital-engineering tooling — SysML 2.0, digital threads, ground-vehicle domain modeling. If nobody on the team has touched systems engineering, that gap will show. This is the specific thing to ask the author about before 22 September.

Why it fits

Model evaluation methodology is squarely in reach for a small team, and the STTR structure means a university partner carries part of the research burden and lends credibility a first-time applicant otherwise lacks.

What makes it hard

STTR mandates a formal research-institution partner with a negotiated agreement in place. Standing that up from scratch in six weeks is unrealistic — pursue this only if you already have a faculty relationship. It also expects evaluation of EO/IR, radar, and RF sensor suites, which is real domain depth.

Critical caveat

The agencies disagree sharply on AI-assisted proposals

This bears directly on how you plan to work. The two relevant agencies have taken opposite positions, and the gap is wide enough to change which one you target.

NSF Permitted

Generative AI may be used in proposal preparation. Disclosure is encouraged but not required; where offered, it should name the tool, describe the use, and confirm a qualified human verified the output.

The hard line is elsewhere: NSF's research-misconduct definition was rewritten to cover fabrication and falsification committed through AI tools. Unverified citations or invented preliminary data are misconduct, not a style problem.

NIH Restricted

Under NOT-OD-25-132, applications or sections substantially developed by AI will not be considered the applicant's original ideas — and will not be considered at all.

NIH deliberately declined to define “substantially”: no percentage, no word count, no rubric. Detection after award can trigger referral to the Office of Research Integrity, cost disallowance, or termination. There is also a six-application annual cap per PI.

The practical reading

Use Claude the way you would use a strong analyst: literature and topic research, structuring arguments, pressure-testing technical approach, drafting code, checking your work against the solicitation. Do not use it to originate the technical idea or generate the narrative wholesale — that is the thing NIH prohibits and NSF reviewers are trained to notice. Verify every citation yourself. Given the ambiguity, HHS is the wrong first target for a team leaning heavily on AI assistance; NSF and DoD are not.

Before you commit

Eligibility, in one pass

  • Ownership — at least 51% held by US citizens or permanent residents.
  • Size — 500 employees or fewer, counted as headcount including part-time and temporary staff.
  • Principal investigator — must be more than 50% employed by the company for the project's duration. Not required at submission, but required at award, so a PI holding a full-time job elsewhere has to be ready to leave it.
  • Work location — performed in the United States. The PI may be a foreign national provided both conditions hold.
  • STTR only — a research-institution partner performing at least 30% of the work, with your company at 40% or more.
Sequence

What to do, in order

  1. Today. Start SAM.gov registration. Nothing else matters until the clock on DLA validation is running — it gates every deadline on the page.
  2. This week, before 22 Sep. Email the Army topic author on ARM26BX06-NV012 and request a call. Ask what the ground-vehicle domain grounding actually requires and what a credible demonstration looks like. You may ask about the requirement; you may not ask them to evaluate your approach.
  3. In parallel. Submit an NSF Project Pitch — four sections, about 10,500 characters total. Two per company per twelve months, so spend them deliberately. Expect one to two months for a response.
  4. 23 Sep – 21 Oct. Write one DoD proposal. Not three. A single well-grounded submission beats a spread every time, and the ranking above is the order to pick from.
  5. Ongoing. Watch the first Wednesday of each month — DoD pre-releases a new batch then, opening the next contact window. If October slips, November is four weeks behind it.
Honest assessment

What would sink this

The NSF trap is technical risk. NSF does not fund straightforward engineering or incremental product build-out. If the hard part of your project is market adoption, integration, or scale-up rather than a genuine scientific or engineering unknown, the pitch gets declined for program fit. “We built a capable application on top of a commercial model” is exactly the shape that fails. The unknown has to be real, and you have to be able to name it in one sentence.

The DoD trap is domain credibility. These topics are written by people with a specific operational problem, and reviewers include the person who wrote it. Fluent prose about agentic architectures will not survive contact with a reviewer who wants to know how it handles their actual data. This is the entire reason the pre-release call matters.

The structural risk is the PI requirement. More than 50% employment at the company is a real commitment, not a formality. Decide who that person is before writing anything — if the answer is unclear, the proposal has a hole in it that reviewers reliably find.

None of these are reasons not to proceed. A first Phase I is a genuinely winnable target for a small technical team, and the current moment — agencies restarting after a six-month lapse, a heavy concentration of AI evaluation and agentic-tooling topics — is a better-than-usual entry point.
